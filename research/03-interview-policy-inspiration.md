### The simulator joke, 25:39 to 27:16

It comes out of a question at 23:10 asking each panelist what they would make if it could not be an action RPG.

- 25:39. Jonathan says a city builder in the Settlers mould, because that is what he plays to relax. He notes Crate Entertainment also pairs an ARPG with a city builder.
- 26:11. Chris says an idea just hit him. He points at the Game Dev Tycoon kind of game where you run a simplified studio, and proposes one about running an online live-service action RPG. You have limited resources and limited time and must split them between fixing bugs, adding content and more. He trails off there. He stresses this is not what his studio is making.
- 26:28. Another panelist cuts in that it sounds like hell. Chris agrees and says that is the point. The player would learn that it is hell.
- 26:37. Chris adds that you would get to read the Reddit posts inside the game. Someone deadpans that this is how they want to spend their spare time.
- 26:45. Chris calls it a piece of art that proves a point. Someone asks whether it does. Chris says he will make it one day.
- 26:52. Another panelist, probably Mark, says he had the same answer ready before Chris spoke, a Path of Exile development simulator.
- 27:01. Two concrete gags follow, speaker unclear. First, players ask for asynchronous trade and the button for it shows a 4,000 hour cost you have to justify. Second, for the first eight years there is a fix-desync button, and if you click it enough Jonathan thinks up a solution and it happens.
- 27:16. The moderator moves on.

That is the whole bit, about a minute and a half. The ingredients they name are scarce time and staff, a split between bug fixing and new content, in-game Reddit reactions, player-demanded features with huge hour costs, and a years-long technical problem that only ends when one specific person has an idea. The tone they want is that the job is hell and the game should make the player feel it.

### Anecdotes about running the studio, by timestamp

Design and decision making:

- 3:47. Jonathan says marketing and game design are closely tied. The team now starts from what it wants to announce on stage, then builds that. Conquerors of Wraeclast began as the question of what nobody would expect them to announce at ExileCon.
- 4:44. Chris says players judge a design on the context it lands in and how they read it, not on what the designers intended.
- 5:37. Mark says the big lesson is not to fear change. Someone in the office or the community always says a thing has been that way forever. Jonathan agrees and says fear drives a lot of AAA development, and a live game makes you worry the players will hate you. One of them, probably Mark, adds that if you must be afraid, be afraid of stagnation.
- 13:44. Erik says the difference between GGG and his card game company is the count of decisions. At GGG each founder, and later Mark, owned a huge area with more decisions than anyone could control.
- 31:25. Asked what surprised him since leaving, Chris says the size of Conquerors. He would have argued for a tiny launch league that nobody would notice. Jonathan chose to go big, which now sets the bar for every later league.
- 33:12. On a Path of Exile 3, Jonathan says maybe, far in the future. After Path of Exile 1 shipped they found a formula for expansions and he calls that the best period. Starting Path of Exile 2 made everything harder because campaign and leagues ran at once. He wants to get back to only making leagues. Someone, unclear who, teases that the previous day's announcement did not sound like an easy life.
- 44:22. Jonathan says the design philosophy never changed. Path of Exile 1's balance got away from them. Mark explains how. Miss a couple of broken interactions, fail to fix them fast, and players start to expect that speed. Then you have to redefine your own philosophy around what people are used to. He names a comet trigger setup and infinite Flicker Strike as Path of Exile 2 cases the team did not foresee.
- 45:25. Jonathan says it is a little sad to build a boss and watch the first player to kill it do so in seconds.
- 49:07. Chris on power creep. Path of Exile has had more than 40 updates and each needs something attractive. Nerfing the best thing draws pushback, so it stays, and the game gets faster every time.

Hiring and people:

- 9:24 to 12:39. Hiring Mark. They urgently needed a QA person and had none. Chris tells it. Mark interviewed as a gamer through and through, and there was a real debate about hiring someone who played games all day. One founder, probably Jonathan, admits he was the worried one because Mark sat at home playing Asheron's Call. Chris had liked one of Mark's builds, a flame totem build, and used it in Build of the Week. That tipped it.
- 10:40. Mark's first-week story. Chris told him a patch needed deploying and asked him to test it, but would not say what was in it. Mark was still a player and the patch nerfed exalted orb drop rates. Chris handed him the office key. Mark came in on Saturday expecting the team and found nobody. He ran a checklist alone, messaged Chris that it seemed fine, and the patch went out. He concluded that responsibility was there for the taking and kept taking more.
- 12:13. Chris says his standard answer to staff who ask how to become senior is to take responsibility and make things happen.
- 19:15. Mark says the QA interview is basically how well you know Path of Exile. He wants players testing the game.
- 38:05 to 40:12. Mark on failure. He gets angry at himself when he ships something bad that he owned. Jonathan talks him down. He learned crisis handling from watching how calm Chris stayed when everything was on fire. The captions garble the example he names. He says the it's-fine meme is true, because panic in a lead spreads to the staff.
- 43:57. Mark's best memory is the first ExileCon. His worst is Chris and Erik leaving.

Money, launch and live-service stress:

- 14:45 to 16:42. Could they rebuild GGG from a garage today? Jonathan says it would be very hard, and that knowing what he knows now he might never have started. They were young and never doubted the game would succeed if they finished it. The only fear was running out of money first. At the time nobody else was making an action RPG, and that was all the positioning they needed. Chris says he started a small studio early last year and chose not to attempt another GGG. He describes GGG as having had an accidental plan for world domination. The game had to be big enough to have players and earn press or nobody would hear of it.
- 41:25 to 43:00. The payment disaster, told by Jonathan. The moment Path of Exile 1 first took money, the payment system broke and people were charged without getting the game. Everyone went to the forums to answer players. They fixed it and refunded everyone. The company was about 18 people. He calls it the first real stress, the point where it stopped being friends in a garage and became a business with customers they could not let down.
- 43:00. Chris on the constant dread of running a live game. Any day can turn terrible without warning. His example is mass database corruption followed by news that the backups failed. He adds that this never happened and praises their backup staff.
- 43:31. Chris recalls a terrible week when someone invented accusations against GGG. He points to a games press article about how a hoax hurt Path of Exile.

AI at the studio, 16:42 to 20:30:

- Jonathan will not force anyone to use AI and will not stop developers who want it. You answer for the work you deliver. He says almost nobody at GGG wants to use it. Sysadmins use it a little, nothing made with it goes into the game, and he saw it used once to find a bug.
- His worry is ownership. Once an LLM writes code, programmers do not want to touch it, and code nobody owns is dead. He can imagine a day when new juniors only know the AI way.
- Someone, probably Jonathan, worries about a flood of AI-made ARPGs a year from now. Chris says nobody told him at the first two ExileCons that they were making an action RPG, and leaves the contrast with this year hanging.

## Part B: GGG's stance on fan content

### Sources checked

1. Terms of Use, one document covering both games. https://www.pathofexile.com/legal/terms-of-use-and-privacy-policy. The privacy part is marked last updated October 2024. The terms carry no date of their own.
2. Developer docs, the sections titled API Policies and Third-Party Requirements and Third-Party Notice. https://www.pathofexile.com/developer/docs
3. Press kit links on official pages and forum posts, listed below.
4. Staff replies on the official forum, listed below.

I found no page from GGG that is a fan content policy, a fan kit or a fan site kit. The terms never use the words fan art, fan site or fan game.

### What the Terms of Use say

- Clause 4, Rights. GGG owns or licenses all copyright, trade marks and other IP in the websites, materials and services. The clause names graphics, logos, text, images, in-game names, characters, locations and virtual items.
- Clause 6, Licence. You get a limited licence to use those things for personal, non-commercial purposes. You gain no ownership. Breaking the terms ends the licence.
- Clause 7, Restrictions. Without GGG's prior written approval you may not adapt, reproduce, store, distribute, display, publish or make derivative works from any part of the website, materials or services outside that licence (7a). You may not modify the game client or its data (7b), use data extraction tools on the website or frame its content (7f), put GGG's name or trade marks in meta tags or hidden text (7g), or reverse engineer anything (7i).
- Clause 8, Recording and Streaming Gameplay. The licence extends to streaming your gameplay and posting gameplay videos, with ads and monetisation as the platform allows. GGG can require you to remove or change the content on request. This is the only place the terms grant a right to make and publish something from the game.
- Clause 11, Image Policy. Images you post on GGG's site must be yours to post, and you give GGG a perpetual royalty-free licence to use them.
- Clause 22, Website APIs. Third-party sites and services may use GGG's official APIs under its API policies.
- Clause 24, Links. A link to GGG's website may not use a GGG logo, trade mark or proprietary graphic without express prior approval.

### What the developer docs say

- The general rule is that GGG's IP cannot be used to generate commercial revenue. The docs give two examples, selling physical items and, in GGG's words, "copying our assets to make other games".
- That sentence sits under the commercial revenue rule. Read literally it describes commercial use. It does not say a free game may copy assets, and it does not say a free game may not. It is the only GGG text I found that mentions its assets and other games together, and the mention is a prohibition.
- Third-Party Notice. You may not present a project as developed or officially authorised by GGG. Using company or game logos in a way that imitates official platforms is given as an example. Every public application should show a visible statement that it is not affiliated with or endorsed by GGG. The exact sentence is on the page.
- GGG can pull API access without notice. The docs call websites and web apps the lowest-risk kind of third-party project. These rules are written for API tools, not games.
- When I checked, the page said GGG could not process new application registrations.

### Press kits

### Staff statements

- Selling fan art, December 2013. Support answered only that the poster needed to contact support@grindinggear.com. No policy was stated. https://www.pathofexile.com/forum/view-thread/674414
- Using the soundtrack in a Twitch tabletop campaign, April 2021. Scott_GGG said the poster was welcome to use the music, on condition that assets made by GGG are not sold or claimed as the poster's own work. He pointed to clause 8 of the terms. https://www.pathofexile.com/forum/view-thread/3088873
- A free fan-made board game using Path of Exile IP, August 2023. The poster asked whether GGG is heavy-handed with cease and desist letters. Nobody replied, staff or otherwise. https://www.pathofexile.com/forum/view-thread/3409179
- Fan art competitions. GGG runs them regularly. The Path of Exile 2 competition posted by Community_Team on 23 September 2025 requires entries to be the entrant's own work and bans AI-generated art. It states no rights taken by GGG and says nothing about reusing GGG's assets. https://www.pathofexile.com/forum/view-thread/3859203

### Allowed, forbidden, unaddressed

Explicitly allowed:

- Streaming and posting gameplay video, monetised or not, subject to takedown on request. Terms clause 8.
- Third-party websites and tools that use the official APIs within the API policies. Terms clause 22 and the developer docs.
- Using the game's music in a stream, unsold and not passed off as your own. One staff reply, tied to clause 8.
- Original fan art, in the sense that GGG solicits and showcases it. No licence text backs this.

Explicitly forbidden without approval:

- Reproducing, distributing, publishing or making derivative works from the website, materials or services beyond personal use. Terms 7a.
- Using GGG's IP to earn revenue, including physical goods and copying assets into other games. Developer docs.
- Presenting a project as official or endorsed, or using logos to imitate official platforms. Developer docs.
- Using a GGG logo, trade mark or proprietary graphic as part of a link to their site. Terms clause 24.
- Extracting data from the client or scraping the website, which rules out pulling art from game files or harvesting it from the site. Terms 7b, 7f and 7i.

Not addressed anywhere I looked:

- Fan games as a category.
- Reuse of official art, logos, music or screenshots in a free, non-commercial game.
- The licence terms of the press kits.
- Parody or satire.
- Use of names such as Grinding Gear Games, Path of Exile, Wraeclast, or of real staff members, in a fan work that uses no GGG assets.

### Answer to your question

On these sources, no. I found nothing that licenses any official asset for use in a fan game, free or paid. The terms make reproduction and derivative works subject to prior written approval, and the one general exception covers gameplay streams and videos. The press kits carry no stated terms and are named and used as material for press and creators. The developer docs name copying assets to make other games as something GGG does not allow, in a sentence about commercial use.

The route the sources do document is to ask. Every staff reply on permissions sends people to support@grindinggear.com, and clause 7 is framed around written approval.

A game with original art and writing that only refers to GGG and its games by name falls in the unaddressed column. The nearest stated norms are the developer docs' notice rules, which are no implied endorsement, no logos that imitate official platforms, and a visible non-affiliation statement. GGG wrote them for API tools.

## Part C: Startup Company by Hovgaard Games

Sources: the Steam store page and Steam's app details, the community wiki at startupcompany.fandom.com, 81 recent English Steam reviews, a 2018 Steam discussion in which the developer pointed players to the wiki, and a 2023 Switch review at thegamingoutsider.com. The wiki was written across several beta versions and contradicts itself in places, so treat exact numbers as indicative. Steam shows the PC version released 10 April 2020 at 12.99 EUR, with 80% of 2,898 English reviews positive.

1. You are the CEO of a web startup. You build one website, grow its user count, and climb past three fictional rivals in your category to rank first.
2. Setup offers a guided career mode or a custom game. You pick a company name, a CEO background and a site type such as social media, streaming, dating, shopping or gaming.
3. The CEO background decides whether your avatar can also work as a developer, designer or manager. Otherwise the CEO recruits.
4. The core loop is a production chain. Staff make components, components become modules, and both are spent to raise feature levels.
5. Higher feature levels raise the ceiling on potential users. Marketing converts potential users into registered ones.
6. Registered users create traffic, traffic needs hosting capacity, and traffic is what you sell to advertisers.
7. Income pays salaries, rent and hosting. Profit buys more staff and unlocks, and the loop widens.
8. Time runs on a workday clock with pause, normal speed and two fast speeds. A skip function jumps the night. Staff only produce during their hours.
9. Developers make technical components. Designers make visual ones. Lead developers assemble components into modules.
10. Sysadmins make the parts that configure servers. Marketers make the inputs for campaigns. Researchers generate research points.
11. Sales executives find advertising deals and each holds one contract at a time. Supporters clear support ticket queues that grow with the user base.
12. Managers run a team against a production plan and add a speed bonus split across their roster of 3, 5 or 8 people. HR managers sit above 3, 5 or 8 managers and set hours and holidays.
13. Recruiters and outsourcing executives are later roles for hiring at volume and for buying or selling components on a market.
14. Every role has three ranks, beginner, intermediate and expert. Higher ranks unlock longer recipes.
15. Training raises a worker's speed toward a personal cap at no extra salary, but they produce nothing while training. Promotion raises both salary and demands.
16. Hiring costs money up front. A job posting is priced by rank and duration, from under 2,000 dollars for a one-day beginner listing to tens of thousands for a week of experts.
17. Candidates show a current speed and a maximum potential speed. A slow candidate with high potential is the cheap, strong pick.
18. Each hire is a small haggle over monthly salary and signing bonus. Lowball offers and long negotiations sour the candidate.
19. Productivity is base speed plus office bonus plus manager bonus, minus a mood penalty.
20. Mood falls every day by an amount that rises steeply with hours worked. Firing someone knocks everyone's mood. A three-day holiday resets it.
21. Intermediate and expert candidates will not apply unless your workplace rating is high enough. Furniture and paid per-head benefits such as a retirement plan or health care raise it.
22. The store page adds that demands grow over time and that rivals can poach unhappy staff.
23. Each component and module has a base duration, from about 2 hours to more than 100, divided by the worker's speed. Starting a module consumes its inputs at once.
24. Production plans are the automation layer. A manager holds target stock levels for each item and the team keeps building toward them. Before managers you queue work per person.
25. Features come in three kinds. Base features raise the user ceiling, revenue features carry ads or subscriptions, and enhancement features add bonuses.
26. Feature slots are scarce, three at the start. One reviewer notes that uneven base feature levels pull user satisfaction down, so you level them together.
27. Frameworks are the platform you license. A better one costs more cash and research points and raises both the slot count and the maximum feature level.
28. Research points are finite until you hire researchers. They unlock features, frameworks and other options.
29. A 20-tier company level gates roles and items. Experience comes from work and contracts, and most roles open in the first ten tiers.
30. Marketing starts with one basic campaign type. Marketers produce copy, ad formats, surveys and tests that power better campaigns, and you tune ad spend against growth.
31. Ad revenue runs through contracts. A sales executive searches for leads in an impressions range. You choose the site, ad type, time slot, guaranteed impressions and length, then haggle the rate per thousand views.
32. Because each executive holds one contract, late-game income scales by hiring very many of them. One reviewer aimed for 200.
33. Hosting starts as rented capacity billed daily. Later you rent a data centre building and fill racks with web, database and cache servers in several sizes.
34. Capacity only counts when the three server types are in balance. You pay purchase, power and bandwidth, and can overclock. Owning servers is far cheaper per unit than renting once you can afford the setup.
35. The office is a grid you furnish. Buildings come in three sizes with caps of 8, 20 and 50 desks, rising rent, and no way back down.
36. Money pressure comes from an opening loan at high interest and a small overdraft. Go past the overdraft and the run ends. A budget screen compares the next 30 days of costs with the last 30 days of income.
37. In career mode an investment firm trades cash for equity and pays out on 25 milestones, from renting an office and reaching 2,000 users up to buying a competitor and ranking first.
38. Falling below majority ownership costs you control. Buying shares back gets dearer as your valuation rises. Starting with no investor is the hard mode.
39. Each rival has a rank and a share price. You can buy their shares, and at full ownership merge their users into your site in the same category. You can also outlast them.
40. A parody microblog shows public reaction to your changes. A countdown to the CEO's retirement runs in the background.
41. Pacing has three phases. The early game is hands-on and short of cash. The middle is about handing work to managers and scaling hosting. The late game is mostly waiting while numbers grow.
42. Reviews agree on the weak points. The most common complaint is repetition and waiting, with several players comparing the late game to an idle game. Others cite per-employee busywork that mods fix, a thin tutorial, site types that differ only in rival names, and an office that is cosmetic beyond its bonuses.
43. The praise goes to the first 10 to 20 hours of the grow and expand loop, the data centre build, and the clean presentation.

Two things I would take from this for a studio sim, as opinion. The production chain plus a manager automation layer is the part players like. The late game dies when nothing new competes for the player's attention, which is the gap the live-service material in Part A could fill.
